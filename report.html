Homework 2: Physician Agency and Payment Changes¶

Victor Li · ECON 372 · October 16, 2026

This notebook works through Homework 2 with the two Medicare datasets from the course. Each numbered section answers one part of the assignment, with the code, tables, and figures behind the answer.

In short, the physicians with the largest percentage drops in epidural injections in 2014 partly recovered in 2015, and the share of high-level established-patient office visits jumped 3.17 percentage points in 2021. Both patterns fit physician agency, but neither one proves the payment changes caused them.

Setup and data¶

The notebook runs from either code/ or the project root. The two CSV files go in data/, which is excluded from git.

A few choices up front. For the injection data, I follow physicians who appear in all three years, require at least 100 injections in 2013, and rank them by their percentage decline in 2014. For the office-visit data, I use the established-patient codes 99211-99215 and weight by the number of services. A physician who is missing from a year is treated as missing, not as zero. The appendix checks how much these choices matter.

Answer. No. Each year's average covers whichever physicians appear in the data that year, so the group being averaged changes from year to year. There are 9,054 physicians in 2013, 8,741 in 2014, and 8,756 in 2015. Of the 2013 physicians, 1,639 are missing in 2014, and 1,326 physicians show up in 2014 who weren't there in 2013. 3. Find large responders using a consistent comparison¶

I make three changes to the raw ranking. First, I keep only physicians who appear in all three years, so a physician who leaves the data isn't counted as cutting injections to zero. Second, I require at least 100 injections in 2013, which removes physicians who barely did the procedure to begin with. Third, I rank by percentage decline instead of the raw drop, so the largest practices don't automatically come out on top. I then take the ten largest percentage declines from 2013 to 2014. The selection doesn't use 2015 or payments, so it doesn't build in a rebound.

Answer. The basic agency model in Chapter 8, section 8.3, where the price is fixed rather than set by the physician, predicts that utilization rises when the price is cut (2014) and falls when it goes back up (2015). The corrected cohort (the 2,891 physicians from part 3) doesn't match the 2014 prediction: average injections fell from 272.69 to 244.67, then slipped again to 240.32 in 2015. The ten largest responders fell in 2014 and partly recovered in 2015.

The most plausible reason for the 2014 mismatch is that a lower fee makes injections less profitable compared with the other services a physician could provide. Chapter 8's two-price extension makes this point by separating what the patient pays from what the physician is paid. In that version, a cut in physician reimbursement reduces injections and an increase raises them, holding other things fixed. The top ten's 2015 recovery fits that story, but the wider cohort kept declining, so payment alone doesn't explain the pattern. Falling demand or a lasting shift toward other treatments could have offset the 2015 increase, and this file can't tell those apart.

Two caveats. The payment column is a simple average of each physician's average payment, not the official fee schedule. And some of the top ten's decline is built in, because they were chosen for having the largest declines.

4. Connect the pattern to agency on the coding margin¶

Answer. Physicians choose the level they bill for each visit, and higher levels pay more. The physician is the patient's agent and knows far more than the patient or Medicare about how complex a visit really was, which leaves room to code up. The 2021 change made that room bigger. It raised office-visit payments and let physicians justify a level with time or medical decision-making instead of the older documentation rules, so billing a higher level became both more rewarding and easier. Agency theory predicts that physicians respond by shifting their coding toward the higher-paying levels, and that is what the data show: the high-level share jumped in 2021 even though total visits stayed below their pre-2020 level. That doesn't have to mean fraud. The new rules may simply let physicians bill the same work at its full level, and CMS still requires visits to be medically necessary and documented.

5. Another explanation and the limit of the claim¶

Answer. Patients may simply have been sicker. Many people put off care during the pandemic, so by 2021 physicians may have been seeing patients with more conditions to manage, which would raise the high-level share even if nobody changed how they code. The file has no diagnoses or severity measures, so I can't separate the two explanations. On top of that, the payment increase and the documentation change took effect at the same time. So the 2021 jump is consistent with a coding response to the policy, but I can't say how much of it the policy caused, or which part of the policy mattered. A stronger claim would need a comparison group the policy didn't affect, or patient-level data on severity.
